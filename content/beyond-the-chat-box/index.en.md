I’ve been using a lot of personal agents heavily lately—and I’ve ended up dropping almost all of them.

From ChatGPT to agents to today’s personal agents, we’ve been through round after round of iteration, yet what sits in front of you is still just a chat box. Some see wrapping a session in a chat GUI as lazy; others think it’s fundamental. Either way, my gut takeaway is simple: **for increasingly capable AI and agents, chat is not a good bridge—and that bridge is bound to change.**

---

The deeper you push an agent inside a chat interface, the more awkward it gets. Two pain points hit me the hardest: **parallel discussions** and **traceability**.

### **Parallel Discussions**

Real work is never single-threaded. Halfway through one task, a new thought pops up or you need to spin off a quick side check, and today’s tools immediately dump an awkward choice on you: **do I keep going in this window, or hit “New Chat” and open a separate session?** Stay put, and the two contexts bleed into a mess; open a new one, and all your background gets cut off. Neither feels natural.

Flip it around: if I actually had a great human assistant, **I would never need to preface a sentence with, “Hold on, I’m about to bring up something new—please open a new channel.”** I’d just throw the thought out there, and they’d know how to judge and route it themselves, instead of making me manage sessions for the system.

### **Traceability**

Chat is a one-dimensional timeline built to scroll past and be forgotten. After working with an agent for a few days, trying to track down a decision you agreed on—or trace how an idea evolved—means wading through hundreds of screens of process noise, dead ends, and tool calls. You’re either scrolling endlessly upward or guessing blindly through a column of identical sidebar titles. The decisions and outputs that actually matter end up buried in a running transcript.

So far, nobody in the industry has come up with a truly good answer to either parallel discussions or traceability.

### **A Deeper Structural Mismatch**

Dig one layer deeper, and there’s an even more fundamental problem with chat:

> Every query a user enters carries a vastly different priority and weight—from a throwaway check on today’s weather to unpacking a critical sales report that unfolds into dozens of follow-up rounds and decisions. **Yet chat as an interface treats everything as the same weight.** Regardless of priority or lifespan, it flattens every interaction into identical bubbles stacked along the same scrolling timeline.

---

I’ve always enjoyed making predictions about the future. Following these pain points forward, I think the next bridge between humans and agents will shift in at least two ways.

One thing I’m sure of: **walking into an AI-native tool and finding nothing except an input box and a chat log is fundamentally wrong.**

**First, input will still be there, but it will expand far beyond typing and speaking.**

I just wrote [I Think I’m Forgetting How to Type](../forgetting-how-to-type/), where voice has already turned my keyboard into a glorified remote control. Yet even with voice, the burden is still on me to narrate reality to the AI. Future inputs will pull in much richer channels—live screen sharing, even ambient environmental context. For an agent, seeing what you’re looking at and the context you’re sitting in—without you manually spelling it out—is often the most valuable context of all.

**Second, alongside input, we’ll get a “dynamic artifact”—and that artifact itself will become the primary trigger for your next input.**

Much like Jarvis in *Iron Man*, it doesn’t sit passively behind a blank text box—it dynamically surfaces the right content at the right moment. **This artifact isn’t necessarily a to-do list or a Kanban board—it’s shaped uniquely for every person and every project**, taking completely different forms depending on who you are and what you’re working on, while continuously evolving as background agents keep working.

It also breaks the blank-box fatigue of today’s tools. You don’t have to rack your brain wondering what to ask next; glance at the living surface in front of you and you immediately see what needs steering or digging into. **The dynamic artifact itself becomes the trigger that pulls you back into the input.**

---

Follow that thread and an obvious question pops up: if dynamic artifacts really take hold, do apps stop mattering?

Plenty of people say apps are dead in all but name and bound to be swallowed by AI, though I’m not so sure. An app is never just a UI—it’s a business model tied to a web of upstream and downstream interests. Technology rarely overturns a business model on its own; markets and businesses always iterate toward their own highest-efficiency equilibrium, and the “ideal interaction” we picture today may not be the end state.

For someone who loves making predictions, this era actually leaves me stumped on a bigger question.

Ten years ago, when Tencent launched WeChat Mini Programs, I had an immediate, unwavering conviction: this is a ridiculously formidable company, and it’s going to be around for a very long time—**because it had the nerve to crap straight on top of Apple’s App Store rules.** And it had the leverage to get away with it. Virtually nobody in China could live without WeChat, whereas not everyone had to use an iPhone. In business, that kind of **irreplaceability** is everything.

Look at AI today by comparison. I’m fairly sure the chat box will be rewritten, yet I have no idea what kind of company can build what kind of product in the AI era to earn the kind of irreplaceability WeChat once had.

To put it in slightly clickbaity terms: if you tried to spot the Moutai or the Tencent of the AI era right now—looking across the entire landscape, there doesn’t seem to be a single one in sight.
